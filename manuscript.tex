\Title{Wind Does Not Disrupt Overwintering Monarch Butterfly Clusters: Direct Empirical Test of a Three-Decade Management Assumption}

%% Simple Summary (required by Insects)
%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\simplesumm{The population size of monarch butterflies in the western U.S. has been declining since the 1980s. This population of butterflies spends the winter months in groves of trees along the California coast. Habitat management in these groves has focused on controlling wind, as wind was thought to negatively affect monarchs. Though this seemed like a good idea, this paper finally tested it. We find that wind itself does not have any negative effects and therefore does not need to be controlled. Instead, we find that sunlight and wind interact. This is likely because sun and wind interact to both warm and cool monarch butterflies. This new finding could change the way in which we manage groves. Instead of managing or suppressing wind, combinations of wind and light should be provided within the groves, so that monarch butterflies can choose among suitable thermal conditions for overwintering.}

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
%% Abstract
%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\abstract{The western monarch butterfly (\textit{Danaus plexippus}) population has declined by over 99\% since the 1980s, making conservation of overwintering habitat a management priority. For three decades, conservation has relied on a Microclimate Hypothesis, including a nested Wind Disruption Hypothesis that associates wind speeds above 2~m/s with roost abandonment. We deployed cameras and wind sensors at two overwintering groves during the 2023--2024 season to track visible cluster size and nearby wind conditions across 80 days. We did not detect a consistently negative association between maximum wind gust and subsequent change in visible Butterfly Index. In the 30-minute analysis, the association depended jointly on approximate local temperature and sun-exposed BI. Gusts were associated with declining BI under cool, sunny conditions and increasing BI under warm, sunny conditions, while conditional wind slopes were not distinguishable from zero when sun-exposed BI was zero. A Next Day Window analysis also supported a conditional wind by sun-exposed BI relationship, with greater model-selection uncertainty. These observations do not provide an exact test of sustained exposure above 2~m/s, but they do not support a simple, universal wind-disruption response under the monitored conditions. The conditional patterns motivate direct tests of thermoregulation as a possible mechanism.}

\section{Introduction}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The distribution and survival of invertebrate species are governed by complex interplays of biotic and abiotic factors. While biotic interactions influence community assembly in part through predation, competition, and parasitism \cite{blois-heulinDirectIndirectEffects1990,laffertyComparingMechanismsHost2013,miller-terkuilePredatorPreyInteractions2022}, abiotic conditions often set the fundamental limits determining where invertebrates can persist \cite{sextonEvolutionEcologySpecies2009,louthanWhereWhenSpecies2015,thomasClimateChangeRange2010}. Temperature constrains invertebrate physiology across all latitudes, from Antarctic midges \cite{everattResponsesInvertebratesTemperature2015} to desert land snails \cite{schweizerSnailsSunStrategies2019}. In invertebrates, temperature correlates positively with metabolic rates and respiratory water loss, creating compound stresses that limit activity windows \cite{chownWaterLossInsects2011}. In addition, via a correlation with temperature, solar radiation can drive both direct physiological impacts and behavioral responses. Examples range from army ants demonstrating habitat-specific thermal maxima tied to insolation exposure \cite{baudierExtremeInsolationClimatic2018}, to terrestrial gastropods climbing vertical surfaces to escape ground-level heat \cite{schweizerSnailsSunStrategies2019}.

Wind is another abiotic factor that can influence invertebrate distribution and behavior through remarkable and diverse mechanisms that vary across species. For example, cockroaches perceive air currents as low as 0.015--0.03~m/s and demonstrate predictable orientation shifts in response to wind velocities \cite{bellSearchAnemotacticOrientation1979}. Mosquitoes' sensitivity to winds as light as 1.0~m/s can effectively ground entire populations \cite{bidlingmayerEffectWindVelocity1995}. Some species exploit wind for dispersal through specialized behaviors, such as spiders' stereotypical ``tiptoe'' behavior which initiates ballooning but only when winds permit controlled trajectories \cite{bonteHeritabilitySpiderBallooning2007}. Some species respond to wind as an environmental stressor requiring active avoidance. For mad hatterpillar larvae, 3~m/s winds are a disturbance cue, triggering movements to stable microsites on branches, and behavioral shifts to leeward sides and abaxial leaf surfaces \cite{leonardExposureWindAlters2016}. Underlying many of these behavioral avoidance responses are the fundamental thermal and hydric effects of wind, as convective cooling from air movement significantly alters both heat and water exchanges between organisms and their environment, particularly when wind interacts with solar radiation \cite{riddellWindNicheThermal2025}. For example, in butterflies, basking is used to warm at cool temperatures. But wing position can be modified to reflect solar energy towards the thorax, or to minimize convective heat loss and maximize solar radiation heat gain of the wing itself, causing antennae, wings and thorax to heat up at different rates and reach different temperature above ambient (reviewed by \cite{munroClimateStrongPredictor2019,tsaiPhysicalBehavioralAdaptations2020,krishnaAirTemperatureDrives2021}). Thus, wind can be recognized as having direct mechanical effects, and indirect thermal and hydric effects through interactions with covariates such as temperature and insolation.

The monarch butterfly (\textit{Danaus plexippus}) offers an exceptional system for studying how abiotic factors shape invertebrate ecology. Late summer environmental cues, such as shortening day length and cooling temperatures, trigger the emergence of a migratory monarch generation \cite{reppertDemystifyingMonarchButterfly2018,goehringEffectsPhotoperiodTemperature2002,hermanJuvenileHormoneRegulation2001,barkerEffectPhotoperiodTemperature1976}. These adults exhibit unique characteristics including reproductive diapause (suspended reproduction), and an elongated-wing long-travel-distance phenotype \cite{barkerEffectPhotoperiodTemperature1976,yangIntrapopulationVariationNatal2016,tuskesOverwinteringEcologyMonarch1978}. The fall migrants collect nectar en route thus building lipid reserves needed for overwintering \cite{hermanJuvenileHormoneRegulation2001,chaplinEnergyReservesMetabolic1982,Urquhart1978Autumnal}. And while Eastern monarchs travel to high-altitude oyamel fir (\textit{Abies religiosa}) forests in central Mexico, our focus, the Western monarch population, migrates to overwintering groves widely scattered along the Pacific coast of California \cite{browerUnderstandingMisunderstandingMigration1995,Urquhart1978Autumnal}.

While overwintering, roughly from mid-October through mid-March, monarchs survive on accumulated lipid reserves and associated thermoregulatory balancing \cite{chaplinEnergyReservesMetabolic1982,mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988}. As ectotherms, monarchs face a fundamental trade-off: maintain body temperatures cool enough through metabolic suppression to conserve energy for the entire overwintering season, yet warm enough to permit for bouts of essential flights \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988,chaplinEnergyReservesMetabolic1982,kammerThoracicTemperatureShivering1970,alonso-mejiaInfluenceTemperatureSurface1992}. Temperatures of 12.7--16.0$^{\circ}$C represent the minimum thoracic temperature required for powered flight \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988} and represent flight threshold temperature for (Eastern) monarchs. Below this threshold, monarchs employ behavioral thermoregulation either through dorsal basking, which can elevate body temperature to flight capability within 30 seconds of sun exposure, or through energetically costly shivering that consumes energy 25 times faster than resting \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988}. Thus ``warm enough'' can be environmentally or physiologically attained, but at significantly different costs to lipid stores. In terms of ``cool enough,'' direct sun exposure can rapidly elevate thoracic temperatures to 33.6$^{\circ}$C, forcing butterflies to adopt sun-minimizing postures or engage in heat-dissipating flights \cite{barkerEffectPhotoperiodTemperature1976}. This energetic balancing means that monarchs selecting overwintering sites must find locations that minimize both sustained freezing exposure (warm enough) and elevated metabolic expenditure (cool enough) \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988,alonso-mejiaInfluenceTemperatureSurface1992,calvertEffectRainSnow1983,andersonFreezeprotectionOverwinteringMonarch1996}.

Our focus on abiotic conditions, but more specifically wind, builds on early studies from Mexican overwintering sites that led Leong \cite{leongMicroenvironmentalFactorsAssociated1990} to propose the Microclimate Hypothesis for western monarchs overwintering in California. This hypothesis posits that monarchs select clustering locations within groves based on a measurable microenvironmental envelope characterized by four key parameters each with a specific benefit: wind protection or speeds below 2~m/s ($\sim$5~mph) preventing cluster disruption, cool temperatures maintaining diapause while minimizing freezing risk and elevated metabolic expenditure, dappled sunlight enabling behavioral thermoregulation, and high humidity or accessible moisture to prevent desiccation \cite{leongMicroenvironmentalFactorsAssociated1990,leongUseMultivariateAnalyses1991}. Wind emerged as particularly critical in Leong's Microclimate Hypothesis, and wind is the only parameter for which there is a proposed parameter value or range (below 2~m/s (5~mph), \cite{leongMicroenvironmentalFactorsAssociated1990,leongUseMultivariateAnalyses1991}).

Leong's initial studies measured wind speeds at trees with and without butterfly clusters, concluding that ``during winter storms, the butterflies clustered on those few trees that offered the greatest protection against winds of 2~m/s or greater'' \cite{leongMicroenvironmentalFactorsAssociated1990}. Though, technically the highest wind speed measured in this study was only 1.66~m/s. Based on these inferred correlations Leong reported that ``butterflies cluster in areas of the grove that offer exposure to filtered sunlight and shelter from strong wind movement'' \cite{leongUseMultivariateAnalyses1991}. In later publications, Leong added anecdotal observations, noting that ``roosting butterflies were blown from their clusters or dislodged by excessive branch movements (shaking) caused by winds exceeding 2~m/s'' and that ``upon dislodgment, the butterflies flew to and resettled on foliage of trees that offered better shelter from strong winds'' \cite{leongRestorationOverwinteringGrove1999}. When storm winds coincided with morning temperatures below 15$^{\circ}$C, ``the dislodged butterflies would be blown to the ground and remain there until temperatures reached flight threshold,'' with ``butterflies littering the ground at the base of roosting trees'' commonly observed after winter storms \cite{leongRestorationOverwinteringGrove1999}.

Though the Microclimate Hypothesis originated from untested anecdotal observations, it still gained widespread acceptance and came to directly inform management guidelines at multiple levels. Leong's 2016 synthesis for California Department of Fish and Wildlife formally established that ``winds $\geq$ 2~m/s are disruptive to the aggregating butterflies by blowing them from their roosting branches or dislodging them by shaking the branches,'' and that when subjected to such winds when above flight threshold temperature, butterflies ``either flew to a more sheltered area of the grove or, if no refuge area was available, abandoned the grove temporarily or for the remainder of the season'' \cite{leongEvaluationManagementCalifornia2016}. Thus, the Microclimate Hypothesis ``made obvious'' that successful overwintering sites would consistently exhibit a specific suite of conditions, including protection from wind $\geq$ 2~m/s. This then implied that specific abiotic parameters should guide habitat management and restoration efforts, a principle now widely adopted in monarch conservation plans \cite{xercessocietyStateMonarchOverwintering2016,jepsenProtectingCaliforniasButterfly2017,peltonMonarchButterflyOverwintering2020,weissRecommendationsRestorationMonarch2008,althouse&meadeinc.EllwoodMesaSperling2023}.

However, empirical testing has gradually dismantled the Microclimate Hypothesis' core assumption of a uniform environmental envelope. When researchers tested whether monarchs select for consistent microclimatic attributes (temperature, light, and humidity) across groves, they found that the realized microclimate varied significantly with latitude and local geography \cite{sanieeHierarchyScaleInfluence2022}. Temperature responses proved more complex than originally proposed, with monarchs avoiding freezing temperatures but selecting the warmest cold temperatures available at their latitude \cite{fisherMultiScaleSpeciesDistribution2024}. Humidity showed geographic variability rather than consistency \cite{sanieeHierarchyScaleInfluence2022}. This geographic variability in microclimate utilization, where selection seems to occur hierarchically across multiple spatial scales rather than for a single environmental envelope, fundamentally contradicts Leong's original hypothesis \cite{fisherMultiScaleSpeciesDistribution2024,sanieeHierarchyScaleInfluence2022}. Of the three Microclimate Hypothesis parameters tested thus far, only light exposure is supported as a potential unifying factor. Given that various research perspectives have been utilized in the consideration of light, successful overwintering sites are known by different light characteristics including consistent patterns of canopy openness \cite{weissForestCanopyStructure1991}, specific shading patterns \cite{ibsenDensityDependenceHabitat2026}, and high intensity light of short duration \cite{sanieeHierarchyScaleInfluence2022}.

Among the four parameters of Leong's Microclimate Hypothesis, the wind disruption component of the Microclimate Hypothesis (hereafter Wind Disruption Hypothesis) stands apart as never having undergone direct empirical testing. Therefore, despite widespread adoption of the Wind Disruption Hypothesis' 2~m/s wind protection threshold in restoration efforts \cite{xercessocietyManagingMonarchsWest2018,jepsenProtectingCaliforniasButterfly2017,althouse&meadeinc.EllwoodMesaSperling2023,u.s.fishandwildlifeserviceMonarchDanausPlexippus2024,weissAlbanyHillMonarch2018,leongEvaluationManagementCalifornia2016}, no study has directly tested butterfly responses to wind exposure, or distinguished whether butterflies actively avoid wind or the hydric or thermic effects of wind, or some other correlated but unmeasured variable(s).

Between the 1980s and mid-2010s, overwintering populations of western monarchs collapsed by approximately 97\% \cite{schultzCitizenScienceMonitoring2017}, with population viability analyses revealing a 72\% quasi-extinction risk within 20 years \cite{schultzCitizenScienceMonitoring2017}. The winter 2018 to 2019 Western Monarch Thanksgiving Count recorded fewer than 30,000 monarchs, a single-year drop of 86\%, representing over 99\% decline from 1980s abundance \cite{peltonWesternMonarchPopulation2019}. The 2020 count documented only 1,901 butterflies, the lowest number ever recorded. The 2024 to 2025 count of 9,119 represents the second-lowest count. The latest count in 2025--2026 of 12,260 is the third lowest on record and demonstrates the population's continued precarity \cite{xercessocietyWesternMonarchButterfly2025}. Declines are associated with multiple interacting stressors including herbicide use and associated breeding habitat loss, pesticide exposure, climate change, and the loss and degradation of overwintering habitat \cite{croneWhyAreMonarch2019,peltonWesternMonarchPopulation2019,oberhauser2026MonarchConservation}. In western monarchs evidence suggests that the overwintering stage represents the most limiting phase of the annual cycle, with declines concentrated during winter and early spring periods \cite{peltonWesternMonarchPopulation2019,mariniDensitydependenceDecliningPopulation2017}. Thus, in terms of Western Monarch conservation priorities, management of California coastal overwintering groves has emerged as the focal point, and testing the Wind Disruption Hypothesis has become critically important.

Testing the Wind Disruption Hypothesis requires addressing a fundamental methodological challenge: isolating wind effects from confounding environmental variables that naturally covary in field settings. Solar radiation is a potential covariate that can trigger butterfly departures through direct heating \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988,kammerThoracicTemperatureShivering1970}. Temperature is a potential covariate known to independently influence activity patterns, with monarchs exhibiting predictable responses as temperatures approach and exceed flight thresholds \cite{barkerEffectPhotoperiodTemperature1976}. Time of day creates inherent activity rhythms related to sun angle and thermal conditions \cite{mouritsenVirtualMigrationTethered2002} especially if clusters reform nightly. Furthermore, wind itself is multidimensional, characterized not only by average speed but also by gustiness and variability \cite{nathanLongdistanceBiologicalTransport2005} that could differentially affect butterfly behavior. A rigorous test must therefore control for potentially confounding factors while examining multiple aspects of wind exposure.
Our primary objective was to evaluate the Wind Disruption Hypothesis. We hypothesized that if wind disrupts overwintering aggregations, increasing wind exposure would predict declines in cluster size over immediate and next-day response windows. We evaluated this prediction while accounting for temperature, time of day, prior cluster size, and sun-exposed BI.

\section{Materials and Methods}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Study Site}

Vandenberg Space Force Base (VSFB, 34.7398$^{\circ}$N, 120.5725$^{\circ}$W) in Santa Barbara County, California, was selected based on: extensive historical plantings of blue gum eucalyptus (\textit{Eucalyptus globulus}) that support overwintering habitat, historical occupation of that habitat, restricted access providing security for long-term equipment deployment, and federal funding that mandated research be conducted on federal lands. VSFB contains thirty documented overwintering groves, with several over the last decade consistently in the top 10 percent statewide based on population abundance \cite{xercessocietyWesternMonarchThanksgiving2025}. Ten locations were selected from the thirty potential sites based on consistent occupancy by monarch aggregations, year-round research access, and first-hand knowledge by Jessica Griffiths, the VSFB Monarch Conservation Coordinator and long-term VSFB coordinator of Western Monarch Thanksgiving Counts \cite{xercessocietyWesternMonarchThanksgiving2025}. We deployed monitoring equipment for two overwintering seasons, with four sites monitored during 2023--2024 and ten sites during 2024--2025.

\subsection{Monitoring Strategy}

Spring Canyon (SC, Western Monarch Thanksgiving Count site 2712, 34.6315$^{\circ}$N, 120.6182$^{\circ}$W) is the most historically reliable and highest overwintering abundance site on VSFB. It is approximately 2.0-hectares of mature blue gum eucalyptus (BGE) ranging to 40 meters in height. A perennial creek runs through the grove, creating a riparian corridor supporting heterogeneous canopy structure, variable tree spacing, and diverse understory vegetation. Surf Road, an infrequently used but paved access road, bisects both the creek and forest canopy. Although only recently (2022) documented, UDMH (Western Monarch Thanksgiving Count site 2822, 34.6719$^{\circ}$N, 120.5950$^{\circ}$W) consistently ranks among the base's highest population sites. UDMH comprises 5.1-hectares of BGE windrows with an understory of scattered low shrubs.
In the 2023--2024 overwintering season two sites were sampled given that monarchs were present. All other sites on base were excluded from analysis due to a lack of monarch butterflies. Unfortunately, the 2024--2025 season coincided with historically low monarch abundance throughout California \cite{xercessocietyWesternMonarchThanksgiving2025}, thus no data are presented for 2024--2025.

\subsection{Field Equipment}
To observe changes in the abundance of monarchs present in clusters in response to wind, we deployed remote monitoring equipment adjacent to butterfly clusters using 15-meter-tall fiberglass telescopic poles (Max-Gain Systems, Inc., Marietta, GA). Poles were anchored on a triangular base, with guy lines connecting the base (anchors) to the pole's top, thus creating stable freestanding structures. Poles were 4--17 meters (horizontal distance) from their cluster. This distance was determined through wireless live-view field optimization to provide the image resolution required by our grid-based counting method (see below). Specifically, this positioning minimized disturbance to monarchs, provided a sufficient field of view, and provided necessary resolution of individual clustered butterflies. In addition, pole placement was often constrained by ground stability relative to the freestanding structure, anchor footprint, clearance requirements, and viewing angles. Deployments near active clusters did not disturb monarchs; no cluster bursts were observed during equipment deployment.

Monarch abundance (cluster size) was monitored using trail cameras (GardePro E7 and E8, Shenzhen, China) modified for near-infrared (NIR) imaging which enhanced clustering butterfly and vegetation contrast \cite{hristovEstimatingOverwinteringMonarch2019}, which in turn improved abundance estimation. Cameras were chosen for durability under extended field deployment, native time-lapse functionality, and modification potential. Modifications exploited an internal filter-switching mechanism by engaging the clear glass filter, disconnecting power to prevent reversion to infrared, and mounting NIR pass filters ($>$850~nm) in front of the native lens. Restricting incoming light to NIR wavelengths exploited the high NIR reflectance characteristic of living vegetation \cite{gatesSpectralPropertiesPlants1965,thajAnalysisOpticalProperties2025} producing black and white images where clustering butterflies appeared as dark masses against bright eucalyptus foliage. Field comparison to human-labeler (see below) confirmed sufficient visual contrast for accurate estimation of cluster size.
Cameras, in time-lapse mode with motion detection disabled, were mounted atop poles and positioned horizontally toward butterfly clusters at roost height. Each camera was positioned with a clear view of the monitored cluster area and remained fixed within a deployment. Camera removal for servicing or any repositioning ended that deployment and initiated a new deployment. The camera sampling interval was determined through statistical validation to balance information content and temporal resolution against battery life and human-labeler data processing capacity. Initial deployments used 10-minute intervals to capture images of changing butterfly abundance. Post-deployment analyses using mixed-effects models and information-theoretic approaches systematically conducted sequential subsampling starting with full temporal resolution (10-minute interval) and progressively testing reduced frequencies. Model comparison, using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), demonstrated that 30-minute intervals provided optimal balance, losing less than 5\% of information (measured by root mean square error) compared to full temporal resolution while reducing image processing workload by 67 percent. Variance comparison analysis and visual assessment of fitted trend lines confirmed that this interval preserved essential time-series patterns including diurnal activity cycles, weather-response dynamics, and multi-day population trends. Battery life constraints and field deployment logistics were consistent with this interval, enabling extended autonomous operation essential for capturing complete behavioral sequences.

To quantify if predicted wind conditions influenced butterfly behavior, wind monitoring equipment (WindLog Wind Data Loggers, Rain Wise Inc., Trenton, Maine) was installed at pole apices thereby measuring wind at roost heights. Wind loggers recorded average wind speed and maximum wind gust at one-minute intervals. This was the shortest interval supported by the sensors which enabled calculation of wind speed minimum, maximum, and variance within each 30-minute photographic interval, and captured gustiness otherwise lost with longer intervals.

Monitoring equipment was repositioned to follow cluster aggregations across a season. To systematically organize our monitoring efforts, we defined discrete cluster monitoring periods as deployment units. Each deployment unit represented a unique combination of cluster location, camera configuration (camera ID, mounting height, and viewing angle), associated wind measurements, and photos for abundance estimates, over a temporal period. This deployment-based structure provided standardized sampling units that accounted for variation in environmental conditions and equipment configurations while treating each deployment as an independent time-series.

\subsection{Image Analysis}

\subsubsection{Grid-Based Counting Method for Images}

To systematically quantify changes in overwintering butterfly abundance, we developed a grid-based protocol that balanced counting accuracy with the practical constraints of analyzing tens of thousands of images. This approach addresses where counts of all individuals would be prohibitively time-consuming, and where estimates of abundance would be a normal alternative, as is typical in monarch field research (e.g., Thanksgiving Count, \cite{xercessocietyManagingMonarchsWest2018}). But, because images were available, and we were not under field conditions, we could be somewhat more systematic. Thus, we subdivided each image using a grid overlay system whereafter human labelers assigned order-of-magnitude estimates (0: no butterflies, 1--9: single digits, 10--99: dozens, and 100--999: hundreds) per cell. Grid dimensions (and thus number of cells) were optimized (most occupied cells contained butterflies in the 10--99 count range) per deployment, and remained fixed within each deployment. Grid dimensions varied across deployments based on camera-to-cluster distance. This standardization balanced classification efficiency (effort) with spatial resolution, minimized cells randomly/erroneously alternating between different order-of-magnitude categories within a time series, and was facilitated with custom software developed using the Electron framework in JavaScript (\url{https://kylenessen.github.io/monarch_trailcam_classifier/}).

\subsubsection{Counting Protocol}

Human labelers, trained on a comprehensive online guide with example images and detailed classification criteria, estimated butterfly abundance within each grid cell using the four order-of-magnitude categories (\url{https://kylenessen.github.io/monarch_trailcam_classifier/}). This optimized efficiency while maintaining consistency across observers. Abundance estimates derived from two-dimensional image planes only, without estimating three-dimensional cluster abundance, structure, or depth. Labelers classified only butterflies that remained visually available within the monitored area and did not estimate butterflies hidden behind vegetation or other butterflies. Labelers also classified cells as receiving direct sunlight when branches or butterflies exhibited additional illumination clearly from direct rather than indirect light. This classification was recorded only for occupied cells and stored separately. Each deployment was classified by one labeler. Because observer identity did not vary within a deployment, observer was not included as a separate random effect. Labelers received ongoing feedback throughout the classification process. All classifications underwent review for common errors including mislabeled cells, incorrect category assignments, and inconsistent counting-criteria application. Direct communication of corrections to all labelers supported consistent protocol application.

\subsubsection{Butterfly Index Calculation}

We calculated a butterfly index (BI) for each frame in a deployment time series:
\begin{equation}
\text{Butterfly index} = \sum_{i} \rho_{i} \times C_{i}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{i}$ represents the number of cells in category $i$, and $C_{i}$ represents the protocol-based count estimate for the number of cells of that category. We used minimum (conservative) category values ($C_{1} = 1$ for category 1--9, $C_{2} = 10$ for category 10--99, and $C_{3} = 100$ for category 100--999) rather than midpoint or maximum values to ensure temporal analyses reflected genuine population shifts on an order of magnitude scale.

Using the same category values, we calculated sun-exposed BI as the BI subtotal contributed by occupied grid cells classified as receiving direct sunlight. Sun-exposed BI is therefore an abundance index for sunlit cells. It is not a count of individually identified butterflies or a measurement of irradiance.

\subsection{Temperature Data Extraction}

Given that temperature may influence monarch activity and potentially confound wind effects, temperature data were extracted from camera-image overlays at 30-minute intervals. Values were not accessible through EXIF metadata, so we developed a Python optical character recognition script using image preprocessing and pattern matching. We manually reviewed deployment-specific time series and corrected transcription errors. The embedded camera sensors were not independently calibrated against reference temperature loggers, and their readings may reflect camera housing and local exposure as well as ambient air temperature. We therefore treat temperature as an approximate local measurement.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

\subsubsection{Data Preparation}
We employed a lag-based framework to capture the temporal dynamics of butterfly responses to environmental changes, comparing the BI between consecutive daytime 30-minute intervals. Observation pairs were constructed by matching BI at time $t$ with BI at time $t - 30$ minutes, applying a $\pm$5-minute tolerance window to accommodate minor temporal variations in image capture. Observation pairs where both time points recorded zero butterflies were excluded as uninformative, reducing the dataset from approximately 2,500 to 1,894 pairs across 115 unique deployment-day combinations. The response variable (change in BI between time points) underwent cube root transformation to achieve approximate normality while preserving directional information: $y = \text{sign}(\Delta) \times |\Delta|^{1/3}$, where $\Delta$ represents the difference in BI ($\Delta$BI). Data analysis revealed generally normal distributions except for bimodality in the $\Delta$BI data from SC deployment 8 where a large cluster abruptly declined to near zero without a corresponding change in any environmental variable. Regardless of the bimodality we retained SC8 in the final analysis for three reasons: to maximize sample size, avoid arbitrary data exclusion, and sensitivity analysis showed that the cube root transformation of $\Delta$BI adequately addressed the distributional concerns, and model selection and parameter estimates remaining consistent whether SC8 was included or excluded.

\subsubsection{Variable Selection}

Predictor variables were selected to test specific hypotheses while avoiding multicollinearity. Maximum wind gust speed during each 30-minute interval served as the primary wind metric, while average sustained speed, modal gust, and gust standard deviation were excluded due to high correlation ($r > 0.75$). Other candidate predictors included average temperature between observation pairs, sun-exposed BI at the earlier time point, time elapsed since the first observation of the day, and BI at the earlier time point. Previous BI was included in one candidate-model framework as a baseline adjustment. Intervals beginning with larger visible clusters could show larger absolute changes, especially losses. Previous BI also contributes mathematically to $\Delta$BI, so its coefficient was not interpreted as a biological response.

\subsubsection{Model Framework}
To test for environmental effects on $\Delta$BI, we fitted generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) using the mgcv package in R. The original set contained 52 named candidates. Two exact duplicates, M20 and M44, were removed before comparison, leaving 50 unique candidates. The set contained parallel frameworks with and without previous BI. Candidates included single predictors, additive combinations, linear two-way and three-way interactions among maximum wind gust, average temperature, and sun-exposed BI, and smooth terms for nonlinear relationships. Four candidates tested tensor-product interactions between maximum wind gust and sun-exposed BI. Random intercepts accounted for deployment and deployment-day variation. An AR(1) structure modeled residual temporal correlation within deployment-day time series. We fitted all candidates using maximum likelihood and ranked successful fits by AIC. The selected model was then refitted using restricted maximum likelihood for coefficient estimation, uncertainty, prediction, and diagnostics.

\subsubsection{Model Validation}

Model assumptions were evaluated using residual distributions, residual-versus-fitted plots, quantile-quantile plots, and residual autocorrelation. Models within 2 AIC units of the minimum were considered similarly supported. Complete fit outcomes and convergence warnings were retained so that unsuccessful models were distinguished from successfully fitted models with weak support.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Window Analysis}
The response time expected for changes in the response variable is unknown. The 30-minute analysis assumes that butterflies can respond within one observation interval, but responses may take longer when temperatures are below the flight threshold or when roosting decisions occur over a longer temporal horizon. We therefore conducted a complementary Next Day Window analysis spanning from the previous day's maximum BI to the current day's last observation. This window approximated functional sunset on the second day, when roosting decisions would have a second chance to be finalized. It varied in duration, with a mean of 29.6 hours.

Daily aggregate metrics were computed for each deployment-day combination, including maximum BI, 95th percentile BI, and mean of the top three BIs. We retained only days with 15--25 daytime photographs to ensure adequate temporal coverage, then constructed consecutive day pairs separated by exactly one day. Pairs where both days had a maximum BI of zero were excluded as uninformative. The response variable, defined as the change in maximum daily BI over the designated dynamic window, underwent signed square root transformation to achieve approximate normality while preserving directional information.
Weather metric variable selection for the Next Day Window analysis began with 19 candidate predictors. Candidates included baseline BI metrics, temperature summaries, wind exposure measures, and cumulative sun-exposed BI. The cumulative sun-exposed BI predictor integrated both exposure duration and instantaneous indexed abundance. We conducted correlation analysis to identify multicollinearity within groups of predictors. Based on this analysis, we selected predictors that minimized redundancy while maintaining interpretability and adequate degrees of freedom relative to sample size. We selected maximum wind gust as the primary wind metric because it was highly correlated with sustained speed and cumulative gust exposure and matched the metric used in the 30-minute analysis. Mode wind gust was excluded because it contained too few unique values and caused model convergence issues. We excluded mean temperature and degree-hours above 15$^{\circ}$C to reduce multicollinearity. The retained predictors were previous-day maximum BI, minimum and maximum temperature, temperature at the previous day's maximum count, maximum wind gust, and cumulative sun-exposed BI. Lag duration was included as a control variable to account for varying window lengths. We required 95\% data completeness across temperature, wind, and daylight observations, calculated as the geometric mean of coverage ratios.

Model selection followed the same information-theoretic approach as the 30-minute analysis. The reconstructed set contained 74 formulas, M1 through M72 and M77 through M78. These included null models, single predictors, additive combinations, and linear or smooth interactions. GAMMs included a deployment random intercept and an AR(1) structure within deployment time series. Candidates were fitted using maximum likelihood and ranked using AICc because the analysis contained 96 pairs. Models with convergence warnings were excluded from ranking. The selected model was refitted using restricted maximum likelihood for estimation and diagnostics.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}

Monitoring at SC and UDMH produced 2,028 daylight observations at 30-minute intervals from November 17, 2023, to February 4, 2024. These observations represented 1,014 observation hours across 115 deployment-days. Of these, 1,894 formed consecutive, informative pairs for the 30-minute analysis.

Maximum wind gusts ranged from 0 to 12.4~m/s, with a mean of 2.2 $\pm$ 1.4~m/s and a median of 2.2~m/s. Temperature ranged from 3.0 to 30.0$^{\circ}$C, with a mean of 14.5 $\pm$ 3.9$^{\circ}$C and a median of 14.0$^{\circ}$C. Sun-exposed BI was greater than zero in 30.2\% of observations ($n$ = 612). Among those observations, sun-exposed BI ranged from 1 to 295 and averaged 16.8.

BI ranged from 0 to 950 per image, with a mean of 80.9 $\pm$ 101.4 and a median of 36. Zero values comprised 3.5\% of observations ($n$ = 71). The BI distribution was strongly right skewed, and the largest mean 30-minute changes occurred during late morning and early afternoon (Figure~\ref{fig:descriptive_bi}).

\subsection{30-Minute Wind Disruption Analysis}

To test whether wind was associated with short-term changes in visible cluster size, we compared 50 unique candidate models. The best-supported model, M16, related 30-minute change in BI to previous BI and a linear three-way interaction among maximum wind gust, average temperature, and sun-exposed BI. All 50 models produced rankable fits. M16 received nearly all candidate-set support (AIC = 8053.95, Akaike weight = 0.9997). The second-ranked model, M40, contained the same three-way interaction but omitted previous BI and was 17.54 AIC units higher.

The fitted relationship between maximum gust and short-term BI change varied with both temperature and sun-exposed BI. The estimated three-way interaction coefficient was positive ($\beta$ = 0.00662, SE = 0.00108, $t$ = 6.11, $p < 0.001$). When sun-exposed BI was zero, wind was not detectably associated with BI change at 10, 15, or 20$^{\circ}$C (all $p > 0.18$). As sun-exposed BI increased, the fitted wind relationship became increasingly negative at 10$^{\circ}$C and increasingly positive at 15 and 20$^{\circ}$C (Figure~\ref{fig:m16_predicted_response}).

M16 accounted for 5.1\% of the variation in short-term BI change. Residual diagnostics showed modest tail deviations from normality and little remaining temporal autocorrelation.

\subsection{Next Day Window Analysis}

The Next Day Window analysis included 96 consecutive-day pairs with a mean interval of 29.6 hours. Daily maximum BI ranged from 0 to 770, and changes ranged from a loss of 376 to a gain of 464 (mean = $-$10.5 $\pm$ 111.6). Maximum gusts within these intervals ranged from 2.0 to 12.8~m/s (mean = 4.5 $\pm$ 1.8~m/s), and cumulative sun-exposed BI ranged from 0 to 1,122 (mean = 139.8 $\pm$ 206.9).

Of 74 candidate models fitted using maximum likelihood, 67 produced rankable fits. M32 ranked first (AICc = 650.01, Akaike weight = 0.614). The baseline model without environmental predictors ranked second ($\Delta$AICc = 3.58, Akaike weight = 0.103), indicating support for M32 but greater selection uncertainty than in the 30-minute analysis. M32 included previous-day maximum BI, window duration, and a tensor smooth interaction between maximum wind gust and cumulative sun-exposed BI. We refitted M32 using restricted maximum likelihood for estimation.

The wind by sun-exposed BI interaction was supported (edf = 6.68, $F$ = 4.10, $p < 0.001$). Predicted changes were positive at low gust speeds and low cumulative sun-exposed BI, negative at similar gust speeds with higher cumulative sun-exposed BI, and positive across parts of the intermediate wind and sun-exposed BI range (Figure~\ref{fig:interaction_wind_sun_nextday}). Sparse regions outside the reliable interpolation area were not interpreted. Previous-day maximum BI had a negative coefficient ($\beta$ = $-$0.0297, SE = 0.0060, $p < 0.001$), whereas window duration was not supported ($\beta$ = $-$0.190, SE = 0.178, $p$ = 0.289). M32 explained 39.7\% of the variation in signed square-root transformed daily change. Its interaction remained supported under alternative BI category values ($p$ = 0.00083--0.00137; adjusted $R^{2}$ = 0.391--0.397). Residual and basis-dimension diagnostics did not indicate major model violations.

\section{Discussion}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------------

For over three decades, conservation practice has operated under the assumption that wind can force butterflies to abandon their roosts, either by physically dislodging them or triggering behavioral avoidance \cite{leongRestorationOverwinteringGrove1999,leongEvaluationManagementCalifornia2016,xercessocietyStateMonarchOverwintering2016,jepsenProtectingCaliforniasButterfly2017,peltonMonarchButterflyOverwintering2020,weissRecommendationsRestorationMonarch2008,althouse&meadeinc.EllwoodMesaSperling2023}. We evaluated whether maximum wind gust predicted declines in visible cluster size across immediate and next-day response windows. We did not detect a consistent negative association under the monitored conditions. In the 30-minute analysis, the association between wind and $\Delta$BI depended jointly on temperature and sun-exposed BI. The Next Day Window analysis also supported a conditional interaction between wind and cumulative sun-exposed BI, although model-selection uncertainty was greater.

\subsection{Evidence Against Wind Disruption}

Neither analysis supported a simple, consistently negative association between maximum gust and subsequent $\Delta$BI. In the 30-minute model, conditional wind slopes were not distinguishable from zero when sun-exposed BI was zero at representative cool, intermediate, and warm temperatures. When sun-exposed BI was greater than zero, increasing gusts were associated with declining BI at 10$^{\circ}$C but increasing BI at 20$^{\circ}$C (Figure~\ref{fig:m16_predicted_response}). The Next Day Window interaction was also conditional and did not show a uniformly negative wind response (Figure~\ref{fig:interaction_wind_sun_nextday}). These changes may represent redistribution among monitored and unmonitored areas, movement into or out of the site, or classification variation. They cannot be interpreted as confirmed arrivals, departures, or physical dislodgment.

Every observation period in the Next Day Window analysis contained a recorded maximum wind gust at or above 2~m/s (range: 2.0--12.8~m/s), and monarch clusters remained visible during the 80-day study period. These one-minute maximum gust records are not equivalent to measurements of sustained exposure above 2~m/s, so they do not provide an exact test of the historical threshold formulation.

Among paired 30-minute observations with butterflies present at the current image time point, maximum gust speeds commonly exceeded the proposed 2~m/s threshold, with 1,074 of 1,856 paired observations (57.9\%) at or above 2~m/s (Figure~\ref{fig:wind_at_clusters}).

\subsection{Thermoregulation as an Alternative Explanation}

Empirical testing has gradually rejected the Microclimate Hypothesis' prediction that overwintering western monarch butterflies have a uniform set of microclimate requirements \cite{fisherMultiScaleSpeciesDistribution2024}
\cite{sanieeHierarchyScaleInfluence2022,ibsenDensityDependenceHabitat2026}
. Rejection of the Microclimate Hypothesis has pointed to the alternative hypothesis that monarch overwintering habitat utilization is driven by physiological and energetic constraints interacting with local conditions \cite{fisherMultiScaleSpeciesDistribution2024}
\cite{sanieeHierarchyScaleInfluence2022}. None of this work has specifically tested physiological and energetic constraints or the thermic and convective environment. But all of it points to the physiological and energetic constraint hypothesis proposed by Chaplin and Wells \cite{chaplinEnergyReservesMetabolic1982} and Masters et al.\ \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988}. Here we briefly summarize salient features of the physiological and energetic constraint hypothesis, while acknowledging that we did not attempt to explore this parameter space either.

Masters et al.\ \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988} demonstrated that cold monarchs actively seek sun. The seeking allows warming from below flight threshold (12.7$^{\circ}$C) to well above it within one minute. Solar basking is effective from ambient temperatures as low as 9$^{\circ}$C. This is inferred to provide an energetic advantage over the alternative warming mechanism, as Kammer \cite{kammerThoracicTemperatureShivering1970} showed that shivering requires 25 to 31 times more energy than resting. However, Masters et al.\ \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988} also found that monarchs avoid sun when thoracic temperatures approach 30$^{\circ}$C. They conclude that monarchs depart sunny cluster sites to avoid overheating and excessive lipid depletion. These patterns, and the inferences that result from them, are what we refer to as the physiological and energetic constraint hypothesis. Here, we outline how the present work also points to the physiological and energetic constraint hypothesis, or possibly a physiological and energetic facilitation hypothesis.

The descriptive data showed the largest average changes in late morning and early afternoon, consistent with previously documented daytime cluster activity \cite{tuskesOverwinteringEcologyMonarch1978,chaplinEnergyReservesMetabolic1982}. Time since sunrise was not retained in M16, so this descriptive pattern should not be interpreted as an independently estimated model effect. Instead, M16 identified a three-way relationship in which the conditional wind association changed with both temperature and sun-exposed BI. This pattern is compatible with a thermal explanation, but it does not establish the underlying physiological mechanism.

Physiologically, what would we predict from a combination of air temperature and sun exposure? We imagine an exclusive effect of heat transfer from air (that avoids shivering) when direct sun exposure is not an option (shade or cloudy). Conversely, we imagine that when clusters are exposed to the morning sun and a clear sky, for a sufficient duration (i.e., one minute), the effect of low air temperatures would be irrelevant. Potentially, the duration of sun exposure required to reach flight temperature could be a function of ambient air temperature. But, overall, we would predict direct sun exposure to be a more significant term than air temperature. Direct sunlight changes thermal status much faster \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988}, could cause a larger absolute change in thermal state \cite{barkerEffectPhotoperiodTemperature1976}, and could even cause thoracic temperature to exceed ambient temperature \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988}. In summary, air temperature alone could define thoracic temperature (under shady conditions), could be preempted by direct sun (especially under cold sunny conditions), or could interact with sun exposure to define behavioral responses under intermediate conditions.

On the cooling side of the equation, the positive conditional wind association under warm, sunny conditions could reflect a thermoregulatory balance in which airflow moderates solar or ambient heat gain (Figure~\ref{fig:m16_predicted_response}). Masters et al.\ \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988} observed that monarchs dissipate heat while gliding via airflow across their bodies. Wind could similarly provide passive convective cooling to stationary butterflies. Riddell and Porter \cite{riddellWindNicheThermal2025} found that wind-driven convective cooling interacts strongly with solar radiation effects in terrestrial organisms. These observations make thermoregulation a plausible hypothesis for the three-way pattern, but thoracic temperature, convective heat transfer, and metabolic expenditure were not measured here.

This interpretation produces testable predictions. Under warm, sunny conditions, monarchs may tolerate or select greater airflow if convective cooling offsets heat gain. Under cool, sunny conditions, the same airflow may slow warming and favor movement away from an exposed location. Under shaded conditions, responses should depend more strongly on ambient and body temperature. These predictions require direct measurements of solar irradiance, thoracic temperature, wind exposure at the butterflies, and movement among roost locations.
If we combine convective wind dynamics, air temperature, direct sun exposure, shading and cloudiness, to the determination of thoracic temperature and lipid thermodynamics, then we begin to consider the parameters of a physiological and energetic constraint model that should better explain cluster dynamics than a Wind Disruption model. We suggest this as a promising line of inquiry for understanding the dynamics of overwinter clustering relative to local environmental factors.

\subsection{Limitations and Context}

Several factors shape the interpretation of our findings. First, our data come from a single overwintering season (2023--2024) when the monarch population was low \cite{xercessocietyWesternMonarchButterfly2025,ibsenDensityDependenceHabitat2026}
. The 2024--2025 season saw near-complete absence of monarchs at our study sites, coinciding with the second-lowest overwintering population on record \cite{xercessocietyWesternMonarchThanksgiving2025}. This absence prevented temporal replication but underscored the urgency of understanding overwintering ecology with the data we have.

Second, the camera-temperature readings were not independently calibrated against reference sensors. They may contain error related to camera housing, solar exposure, or sensor-specific bias. Because temperature participates in the M16 interaction, such error could attenuate or distort the estimated conditional relationship. The temperatures used to display model predictions should therefore be understood as approximate local readings rather than precise measurements of ambient or butterfly temperature.

Third, cameras remained fixed and unobstructed within deployments, but wind-driven branch movement could redistribute a visible aggregation among grid cells. A cluster might therefore cross order-of-magnitude category boundaries without a corresponding change in actual abundance. This process could introduce short-term error into 30-minute $\Delta$BI and may occur more often during windy periods. Sensitivity to alternative category values showed that the interaction did not depend on the chosen lower-bound values, but it cannot eliminate this source of error. Formal inter-observer agreement and validation against independent counts were not possible because each image was classified once. The observer sensitivity analysis assesses robustness to average observer differences, not classification agreement.

Fourth, though we did not observe it, we do not dispute that wind can dislodge butterflies from a cluster. We see at least two conditions under which disruption could be possible or even likely. The Wind Disruption Hypothesis was developed during an era of massive aggregations containing hundreds of thousands of individuals, where many butterflies attached only to other butterflies in multi-layered formations \cite{leongMicroenvironmentalFactorsAssociated1990,browerMonarchButterflyClusters2008}. In contrast, our study observed relatively small clusters where a larger proportion of clustering butterflies could maintain direct contact with eucalyptus substrates. Thus, if substrate attachment provides greater wind resistance than butterfly-to-butterfly attachment, then wind might affect these different clustering configurations differently. It is thus possible that the Wind Disruption Hypothesis may be more accurate under extreme densities as reported by Alonso-Mejia et al.\ \cite{alonso-mejiaInfluenceTemperatureSurface1992}. Those authors report that monarchs can be dislodged by the wind. They also report that monarchs can be dislodged by rain. Freezing conditions can be survived by monarch butterflies, to temperatures as low as $-$7 or $-$8$^{\circ}$C \cite{alonso-mejiaInfluenceTemperatureSurface1992,andersonFreezeprotectionOverwinteringMonarch1996}, respectively. Though if conditions are wet instead of dry, survival at this temperature is reduced by 50 percent \cite{alonso-mejiaInfluenceTemperatureSurface1992}. Also at these temperatures, shivering and then crawling up vegetation will significantly increase survival \cite{andersonFreezeprotectionOverwinteringMonarch1996,mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988} as monarchs avoid the lowest temperatures (at substrate level). Like crawling, if monarchs are under dense foliage their body temperature approximates ambient, but with exposure to the night sky body temperature falls in proportion to exposure \cite{andersonFreezeprotectionOverwinteringMonarch1996}. This exposure reduces survival and even more so if wet instead of dry \cite{andersonFreezeprotectionOverwinteringMonarch1996}. Similarly, wind driven convective cooling can reduce survival and even at slow wind speeds \cite{kingsolver_thermoregulation_1983}.
Finally, M16 explained 5.1\% of short-term variation, and M32 explained 39.7\% of Next Day Window variation. M32 also had appreciable model-selection uncertainty relative to the baseline model. We therefore interpret the results as a failure to detect a consistent wind-disruption response under the monitored conditions, rather than evidence that wind disruption cannot occur in other settings.

\subsection{Unexplained Factors of Variation, Resolution and Replication}

Substantial variation remained unexplained at both response windows. The higher adjusted $R^{2}$ for M32 should not be treated as proof that still longer windows would perform better because the analyses used different response definitions, transformations, sample sizes, and predictor summaries. Evaluating additional response windows with direct thermal measurements could help determine the temporal scale at which environmental conditions best predict cluster change.

Social dynamics may also contribute to unexplained variation. Monarch aggregation can involve density-dependent settlement or movement \cite{wells_importance_1990,freyClusteringPatternsMonarch1992,berdahlEmergentSensingComplex2013}. However, the negative previous-BI coefficients in our change models cannot establish such a mechanism because previous BI is mathematically coupled to the response. Testing social effects would require an analysis designed specifically to separate density dependence from regression to the mean and measurement error.

Finally, testing these patterns across the broader overwintering range would establish their generality while allowing the capture of both temporal and spatial variance. Sites with different tree species, latitudes, and especially population densities could reveal whether wind responses vary with clustering configuration or if our findings represent fundamental aspects of western monarch overwintering behavior.

\subsection{Implications for Conservation}

Our findings question whether a single wind threshold adequately describes overwintering habitat use at the two monitored groves. They do not establish that wind protection is unimportant elsewhere or justify reducing existing protections. Instead, they support evaluating wind together with temperature, solar exposure, cluster density, and grove structure when developing and testing future management criteria.

\subsubsection{Expanded Habitat Availability and Population Limitations}

Are we observing limited suitable habitat for overwintering western monarchs? Or, with western monarch populations at historically low levels, are there too few butterflies to occupy all suitable spaces? The absence of wind disruption despite frequent threshold exceedances indicates that suitable habitat within existing groves may be more extensive, and less wind limited, than currently recognized, at least at the modern temperatures and population sizes experienced by Western monarchs. Therefore, groves previously dismissed due to wind exposure might support clusters under larger population conditions, simply because there would be butterflies to occupy them.

\subsubsection{Simplified Management Requirements}

Current management frameworks recommend protecting buffer zones around clustering sites (e.g.: up to six tree-heights away from cluster trees, Althouse \& Meade, Inc.\ and Creekside Science \cite{althouse&meadeinc.EllwoodMesaSperling2023}) to ensure adequate wind protection. This would equal 240-meter buffers for 40 meter tall cluster trees. Such buffers would derive substantial complexity, and potential management hurdles, in coastal California where overwintering sites often involve diverse stakeholders. For example, in Pacific Grove, California, cluster trees are within meters of a neighborhood fenceline, and a cluster tree extends over a hotel parking lot (F.X.V.\ pers.\ obs.), such that more than half of the buffer zone would be on residential and commercial property. Yet, trees adjacent to groves, now considered essential for wind breaks, may contribute far less than currently thought. Thus, simplifying buffers would allow focusing conservation efforts on grove structure rather than on controlling wind in surrounding areas. More importantly, if light and wind (and maybe temperature) are all inter-related through butterfly thermal physiology, then a focus just on wind would be myopic.
Just as we suggest that researchers view overwintering monarch butterfly biology through the Physiological and Energetic Constraint Hypothesis, we suggest that land managers view overwintering habitat through the Physiological and Energetic Facilitation Hypothesis. This later hypothesis encourages viewing the overwintering grove as providing thermal resources. These resources would be locations within the overwintering grove where heating and cooling mechanics can interact. These locations would be where monarchs could find: shelter from rain and dew, deep shade, convective cooling, wind assisted cooling, patchy solar exposure, solar basking, and combinations thereof. The configuration of these resources is known to be important, as Sears et al.\ \cite{sears_configuration_2016} find that ectotherms can regulate more accurately, and at a lower energetic cost, when thermal resources are dispersed in the habitat rather than when they are clumped. Meaning that spatial heterogeneity of shade, wind and sun would be the management target and not wind abatement.

Nonetheless, buffer trees and surrounding forested areas should be preserved, not because of their wind abatement capacity, but because buffer areas represent potential raw material for future habitat creation. There is a fundamental asymmetry because large mature trees can be readily removed, but they cannot simply be replaced as such. They require many decades of growth to reach the heights and canopy structure that characterize successful overwintering groves. Losing or prematurely removing these trees is especially consequential because no amount of funding can accelerate growth's timeline. Prudent conservation therefore favors preserving existing large trees, which can potentially be converted to functional overwintering habitat in the future through informed canopy management.

\subsubsection{Proactive Habitat Creation Through Canopy Management}

The current practice of wind protection employs planting trees and waiting decades for maturation, as well as the maintenance of healthy, mature specimens. If monarchs respond primarily to temperature, light and wind, and thermal patterns rather than strict wind thresholds, strategic canopy modification for light-based management might enable shorter-timeframe interventions.

Real-world examples support this possibility and suggest that we return to some of the early light-based work of Weiss and Leong, which has somehow been disregarded for wind-based inferences of the same work. For example, Weiss et al.\ \cite{weissForestCanopyStructure1991} found that successful overwintering sites maintain consistent canopy openness around 20\%, while unsuccessful sites show greater variability. At Monarch Lane in Los Osos, California, strategic canopy opening resulted in monarchs reoccupying an overwintering site \cite{leongRestorationOverwinteringGrove1999,xercessocietyWesternMonarchButterfly2025}. At Andrew Molera State Park in Big Sur, California, clearing an overgrown grove increased butterfly counts from zero to over one thousand (E.\ Pelton, pers.\ comm.). Conversely, tree planting at Pacific Grove required approximately 15 years of canopy maturation before monarchs returned (S.\ Weiss, pers.\ comm.). These patterns suggest that appropriate canopy structure, including carefully designed openings creating dappled light, is known to be successful, and can attract butterflies far more quickly than waiting for tree growth alone.

Unoccupied eucalyptus stands or overly dense groves might thus be converted to functional habitat through selective thinning. However, additional research is needed before implementing large-scale canopy modifications, particularly at established overwintering sites. But in the end, managers might proactively create suitable conditions by strategically opening existing canopies at appropriate locations (for example previously occupied but currently unused sites), representing a significant departure from previous approaches that focused almost entirely on wind protection and tree growth. This proactive and strategic approach is one of site restoration and could increase resilience for overwintering monarch butterflies.

\subsubsection{Long-term Optimism Despite Current Uncertainty}

While this study reduces certainty regarding some aspects of overwintering ecology, the long-term trajectory points toward management practices that may be simpler to implement, are grounded in empirical evidence, and potentially are outright effective instead of simply somewhat effective. The long-term trajectory suggests managers could be offering more to overwintering monarchs than just wind abatement. Our suggestion, like those of others, is that adding access to light and wind should provide significant thermoregulatory and energetic benefits. But if we can use the Wind Disruption Hypothesis and the last 30 years as a cautionary tale, then clearly predictions from the physiological and energetic constraint hypothesis (\textit{sensu} Chaplin and Wells \cite{chaplinEnergyReservesMetabolic1982} and Masters et al.\ \cite{mastersMonarchButterflyDanaus1988}) should be rigorously tested.


%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusions}
%% ------------------------------------------------------------------

Across the wind conditions measured at two overwintering groves during one season, we did not detect a consistent negative association between maximum wind gust and subsequent visible cluster change. The 30-minute association depended jointly on wind, approximate local temperature, and sun-exposed BI. The Next Day Window also supported a conditional wind by sun-exposed BI relationship, with greater model-selection uncertainty. Because one-minute maximum gust records do not measure sustained exposure above 2~m/s, this study does not provide an exact test of the historical threshold formulation.

The conditional patterns are compatible with a thermoregulatory explanation, but we did not measure thoracic temperature, solar irradiance, convective heat transfer, metabolic expenditure, or lipid depletion. Thermoregulation should therefore be treated as a hypothesis for future testing rather than as a demonstrated mechanism.

These findings arrive at a critical moment for monarch conservation. With western populations at historic lows, assumptions about habitat requirements deserve continued scrutiny. Our results do not justify removing existing wind-protection measures, but they do support testing whether management criteria based on a single wind-speed threshold adequately represent the environmental conditions used by overwintering monarchs. Conservation efforts should continue to preserve existing groves while direct physiological and multi-site studies evaluate alternative management criteria.

\section{30-Minute Wind Disruption Analysis: Candidate Models}
\label{app:30min_models}

This appendix documents the 52 named candidate models defined for the 30-minute wind disruption analysis. M20 duplicated M17, and M44 duplicated M41. These duplicates were removed before comparison, leaving 50 unique candidates.

\textbf{Model Structure.}
All models used Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMM) with the following structure:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Response variable}: $\sqrt[3]{\Delta\mathrm{BI}}$ (cube root of BI change between consecutive 30-minute intervals)
\item \textbf{Random effects}: Random intercepts for deployment and deployment-day
\item \textbf{Correlation structure}: AR(1) autocorrelation within observation days
\item \textbf{Estimation method}: Maximum likelihood for model comparison, followed by restricted maximum likelihood for the selected model
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Variable Definitions.}
\texttt{total\_butterflies\_t\_lag}: Previous butterfly index (lagged);

\section{Next Day Window Analysis: Candidate Models}
\label{app:nextday_models}

This appendix documents the 74 candidate formulas reconstructed for the Next Day Window analysis. The defined candidates were M1 through M72 and M77 through M78. Model identifiers M73 through M76 were not defined in the original analysis.

\textbf{Model Structure.}
All models used Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMM) with the following structure:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Response variable}: signed $\sqrt{|\Delta\mathrm{BI}_{\max}|}$ (signed square root of change in maximum daily BI between days)
\item \textbf{Random effects}: Random intercept for deployment site
\item \textbf{Correlation structure}: AR(1) autocorrelation for temporal dependence
\item \textbf{Estimation method}: Maximum likelihood for model comparison, followed by restricted maximum likelihood for the selected model
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Variable Definitions.}
\texttt{max\_butterflies\_t\_1}: Previous day maximum butterfly index (tested as smooth or linear);
\texttt{lag\_duration\_hours}: Weather window duration (tested as smooth or linear);
\texttt{temp\_min}: Minimum temperature over the Next Day Window ($^{\circ}$C);
\texttt{temp\_max}: Maximum temperature over the Next Day Window ($^{\circ}$C);
\texttt{temp\_at\_max\_count\_t\_1}: Temperature when max count occurred ($^{\circ}$C);
\texttt{wind\_max\_gust}: Maximum wind gust in window (m/s);
\texttt{sum\_butterflies\_direct\_sun}: Cumulative butterflies in direct sun;
$s()$: Thin-plate regression spline smooth term;
$ti()$: Tensor product interaction smooth.

Models were generated programmatically across seven categories. The first number in parentheses indicates models with smooth baseline controls; the second indicates linear baseline controls.
